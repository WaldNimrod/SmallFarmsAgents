Cover Crops
WHY GROW THEM
Cover crops or “green manure” as they are often referred to, are an essential part of
creating healthy soils in the market garden. They can play different roles and are used
in different contexts, but for the most part we rely on them to protect the ground from
soil erosion, improve soil structure and feed our soil web.
Cover crops are not a magic bullet for soil fertility (even if they can fix nitrogen) and
will not replace other amendments like compost and natural fertilizers, but they are an
important « tool » to grow abondant and beautiful crops all while sustaining fertility
over time.
4 main functions of covers in the market garden:
• Protection effect: The soil needs to be covered in winter times when vegetables
are not being grown. This prevents erosions of your topsoil.
• Structuring effect: The roots system of cover crops will dig your soil filing all the
cavities and create an uncompacted soil profile.
• Fertilizing effect: Legumes fertilize crops with N without adding phosphorus to the
soil.
• Feeding effect: The roots are feeding your soil web , in turn, fuels you systems.
Plant to grow deep rooting crops at least once in your calendar year.
For these effects to occur, a cover crop must set on a period sufficiently long. As a
general rule of thumb, your cover crops should have at least 8 to 10 weeks to develop
and propagate. Another 7 to 10 days is also needed for mowed down cover crop
residues to be digested before planting into.

To get the most fertilizing action out of a leguminous green manure, it helps to keep
the following two pieces of information in mind:
Firstly, the best time to mix in a green manure is just before it flowers. It is at this stage
that the plant has stored its maximum nitrogen and will make the greatest
contribution to soil fertility. With the crop still young and tender, it will decompose
quickly and easily, in time for the following crop.
Secondly, you need to know that legumes do not fix nitrogen from the air by
themselves. The fixing is actually done by bacteria of the genus Rhizobium, which
form nodules on the roots of the leguminous plants and permit the exchange to take
place. However, these bacteria may not necessarily be present in the soil if legumes
have not been grown there for some time. Regardless, it is a good idea to inoculate
your legume seeds with the proper rhizobia.

COVER CROPS AND LIVING SOILS
Our choice of growing cover crops and our strategies to deal with them should be
understood in the context of working with living soils. Remember that the biota in the
ground (Ie. all the living organisms in the soil) is the main driving factor of all fertility
available for your crops. The more active and alive your soil is, the more fertile it is, the
better your crop will be.
Also remember that the ecosystem (ie. the habitat) in which this biota lives is fragile
and in many ways influence by your farming practices. Soils that are compacted and/
or continuously tilled cannot allow for earthworms (amongs others) to play a crucial
role in fundamental biological processes to makes healthy soils.
Other basic principles for creating optimal life in soils all interact together and it's
convenient to understand these components as hierarchical levels of a pyramid. The
lowest levels conditions must be satisfied before higher up conditions can be met.
The diagram on the next page illustrates the stacking role that each strategies plays in
the overall fertility of your soil and can help you prioritize where to start from.
Getting back to cover crops, what’s important to understand is that when we stop
tilling, organic matter (the food of the soil web), doesn’t get as easily brought down
to the lower layers of your soil. A rototiller or plow does that very well and explains
why most growers « till under » their cover crops.
