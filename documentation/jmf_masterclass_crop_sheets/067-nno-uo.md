Irrigation Management

→ Root depth : Shallow ( 6”-24” ) to Medium ( 24”-40” )

→ General : Sensitive to disturbance. Needs consistently moist soil. Lack of water when
fruits are developing will cut down on production. Favor drip irrigation and avoid
overhead watering since cukes are susceptible to fungal disease, which spreads
through wet leaves.

Cucumber plants require lots of water when in full production during the summer :
3 to 4 litres per plant per day. The exact amount will depend on the stage of growth
and outside temperatures. Generally, the 9am to 2pm rule should be followed :

→ Schedule 1 to 5 irrigation cycles per day. These cycles should be done
between 9am and 2pm. Start irrigation 2 to 3 hours after sunrise ( giving
the plants time to get active ) and stop irrigation 5 to 6 hours before
sunset ( avoiding too much water in the rootzone at night ).

We also program 3 irrigation schedules on the timer. The frequency of watering cycles
will depend on outside temperatures;

Sunny : 3 to 5 cycles per day

Partially sunny : 2 to 3 per cycles day

Cloudy : 1 to 2 cycles per day

To manage this, we use a watering clock with different start times and schedules.
The irrigation is adjusted each morning according to sunrise and sunset times and
weather. It takes only a few seconds per day to adjust the water clock.

The length of each irrigation pulse has to be adjusted to ensure that the plants are getting
the right amount of water. A good way to test this is to sample soil in the greenhouse at
the end of the day and first thing the next morning - take a handful of soil at each point,
and judge the moisture content. Both should feel about the same.

BEFORE TRANSPLANTATION
Make sure that the steel support wire is installed and ready to go before you set out
your transplants.

The success of a cucumber transplant is largely a result of both soil and air temperatures.
Soil temperatures have to be above 62 °F ( 17 °C ) and the ambient temperature ( day and
night ) should be above 61 °F ( 16 °C ). Failure to maintain the plants to such minimums will
shut the plants down and adversely affect the root uptake. It will also increase the risk
of soil borne diseases like pythium. Cucumber transplants are sensitive, and should never
be allowed to become stressed for water or nutrients. A bad start is a set-up for future
problems.

Make sure all furnaces are operational, with back-up ready to go. Plan to measure soil
temperatures 2 weeks prior to the transplanting date. If too cold, cover beds with clear
plastic – a few sunny days will help warm up the soil. In cold climates, installing radiant

WHEN TRANSPLANTING

Broadfork the beds.

Mark the spacings every 12’’- 30cm ( Lebanese ) or 18”- 45cm ( English ), then dig the holes
using a round shovel ( ±5” diameter ). Add some compost in each hole and a handful of
chicken manure ( 5-3-2 ) or another fast-acting, balanced natural fertilizer. Then, fill the
hole with water using a hose. The mixture should become a muddy matter.

On our first planting in the spring, when the soil is not warm, we sometimes use a
rototiller and incorporate all of our amendments directly in the top 8’’- 20cm of the soil.
Tilling will add lots of oxygen in the bed, warming it up.

Place cucumber seedling in front of each hole, making sure not to mix up the cultivars.
Start by delicately extracting the seedlings from their pots and placing them gently into
the hole. The soil level on the seedling should end up being level with the soil. Cucumber
seedlings have sensitive roots. Handle them carefully, press lightly on them to increase
roots/soil contact and don’t bury their collars.

Attach one piece of twine to each plant using tomato clips and tie it to the overhead wire.
The twine should not be completely tight. Wind the plant along the twine and prune every
sucker, tendril and fruit. At that point, the plant is not well established enough to support
any fruit.

Install 4 drip tape lines, but activate only the 2 closest to the plants. This way, we avoid
over-mineralizing the rest of the bed and are keeping the “pantry” abundant for another
few weeks. 2 weeks later, open the 2 outside drip tape lines, bringing the total to 4 drip
tape lines per 30’’ ( 75 cm ) beds. SEE THE IRRIGATION SECTION FOR WATERING ADJUSTMENTS.

Install white on black plastic between rows. The tarp helps keep the fertilizers moist and
the soil dark so that the earthworms, woodlouse and other critters may gather to feed
24 hours a day within the soil. On the flip side, its white surface serves to increase light
reflection onto the plants, thereby encouraging photosynthesis. SEE APPENDIX 6 FOR DETAILS.

For 7 days after transplanting, maintain a constant temperature ( day and night ) of
73 °F to 77 °F ( 23 °C to 25 °C ). This will encourage vegetative growth and root retake.

After the first week, maintain the same daytime temperature, but lower the night
temperatures to between 70 °F and 73 °F ( 21 to 23 °C ).

The best way to heat your greenhouse is to install venting tubes in the pathways
between each bed ( see video ). To help prevent fungal problems, we leave the blowers
to ventilate 24/7. This is done by wiring the blower of your furnace so that it’s always
running.

When cucumber harvest starts, try to maintain daytime temperatures in the
greenhouse between 70 °F to 75 °F ( 21–24 °C ), and night temperatures between 63 °F
and 68 °F ( 17–20 °C ). A good twenty-four-hour average for cucumbers is 70 °F ( 21 °C ).
If possible, avoid temperatures above 86 °F ( 30 °C ) and in the event of hot days, go on
the side of watering too much, rather than not enough. It's OK for cucumber leaves to
wilt during hot afternoons, as long as they perk back up at the end of the day. If they
don’t, more water or ventilation may be needed.

As for humidity, the optimal rate for greenhouse cucumbers is between 65% and
80%. An environment that is too humid will lead to problems related to fungal disease
and to poor floral fertilization. Excess or lack of humidity will strongly influence
crop productivity.

To control excess humidity ( 80% or higher ), the only way is to heat and ventilate
at the same time. Waves of dehumidification can be programmed and their intensity
depends on outside temperature and the level of excess humidity. During those waves
of dehumidification, a general guideline is to set the temperature setpoint at +30 °F
( 1 °C ) above your desired set point and to ventilate by opening the rollups and roof
( if available ). If it’s cold outside, heat and open for less time than if it's hot and sunny
outside. Repeat this operation ( heat and ventilate - pause - repeat ) until the humidity
is back to below 80%.

Also note that, to produce regularly, cukes need a great amount of light. In
greenhouses where the climate is temperate, cucumbers will grow faster in summer
when daylight lasts 17 to 18 hours, compared to fall when daylight decreases
to 12 hours and less.

Pruning cucumber plants needs to be done at least once a week, but preferably twice
a week. Otherwise, the plant tends to set more fruits than it can support, which ultimately
leads to new fruits being aborted.

For both English and Lebanese cukes, in the first 2 weeks, or before the plant reaches
2’ ( 60 cm ) high, all the fruits and suckers should be pruned off the plant. By limiting
the number of fruits on the plants while it’s establishing its roots, you will have higher
total yields.

After the first two weeks, follow these simple pruning guidelines :

→ English cukes : Prune to leave 1 fruit every 2 nodes and remove all tendrils + suckers.
→ Lebanese cukes : Leave all fruits, but remove all tendrils + suckers.

Lebanese cucumbers are more vigorous than English, hence the reason why their fruits
are not removed.

In both cases, wind the plant around the twine clockwise. We prefer winding the plant
around the string, instead of using clips to the trellis. It is faster and eliminates having
to carry around material requiring manipulation.

When the head of the plant reaches the wire, attach it with a clip and train it to grow
along the wire to its right. Until the main head reaches the next plant, let all the suckers
grow – these will form new heads that will be shooting down. Once the main leader
reaches the next plant, clip it to the wire for support, and let it grow downwards.

By that time, you should have about 3 suckers that have developed into new heads. These
are growing downwards ( from the top wire heading towards the ground ). Let them grow
down freely to about chest height. Then, in one passage, cut off the heads of every plant.
The plants will now finish maturing the fruits that are already set on the plant. Ultimately
after 10 to 12 weeks, production will decline considerably. It is therefore time to replace
the plants with a new succession. To make sure we don’t have a gap in our production,
we plan to transplant a new succession 2 weeks before the last one stops producing.

Similar to removing suckers, pruning leaves allows the plant’s energy to go toward
fruit production, instead of excess vegetative growth. Removing the lower leaves also
increases airflow, which can reduce the incidence of disease.

You can begin pruning the bottom leaves when the head of the plant reaches the top
of the trellising wire. Remove the lower leaves on the first 12” ( 30 cm ) to increase
airflow. At that point, the plant should have ±18–20 leaves and you can remove 1–2
leaves every second week. The leaves most appropriate to remove are those on the

Pest control is a major part of successful greenhouse cucumber growing. How to deal
with the problem will depend on many different variables, but prevention and observation
should be your primary tools. The aim is to favour a plant–positive approach to pest
management, avoiding pesticide use when possible and working with predatory insect
introduction instead. It’s the way to go, but it requires preparation and advance planning.

In our climate, certain insects are sure to become problematic if not handled preventively
with the help of certain infrastructure :

Cucumber beetles and the damage they do by spreading bacterial wilt can be mostly
avoided by installing insect screens in the openings of the roll ups. Highly recommended.

Spider mite populations are greatly reduced by keeping humidity levels in the greenhouse
above 60%. We achieve this by adding fogging misters to the cucumber greenhouse. These
are set on a simple watering clock that will have them turn on between 10am and 3pm
every hour ( or half-hour on very hot days ) for 30 seconds to 1 minute. Activate these
misters only on sunny days to avoid wet leaves on cloudy days, which leads to higher risk
of diseases like powdery mildew.

Diseases are also very context–dependent. The first line of defence is to use cultivars
that are disease-resistant. Using varieties that are resistant to both downy and powdery
mildew is a must.

In our experience, fungal problems ( Alternaria and Cercospora leaf spot ) are more
effectively treated by spraying biological-based fungicides, rather than other certifiable
sulfur solutions. A bio-fungicide like Serenade ( which works with Bacillus subtilis )
contains living bacteria or fungi that help reduce pathogenic microbes through competition
or antagonism. Such biological products don’t harm beneficial insects and other non-
target organisms. In the case of a serious fungal problem, microfine sulfur can be used,
but be sure to carefully read the produce indications since sulfur can be toxic to plants
if it is not used correctly.

Pythium and the damage that it does to the transplants is often a result of soil that was
not warm enough when the plants went into the ground. A product like rootshield ( a
biological fungicide ) can also be very effective in preventing pythium outbreak, especially
with the first planting of the season. We recommend one preventive application in
the nursery, just before the transplant. Trichoderma ( the active fungus ) colonizes the
cucumber roots and protects them from a Pythium attack.

Removing sick plants and cleaning your hands when handling them is also vital in
protecting against bacterial or viral infections. Diseased plants should not be put in the
compost, but in the garbage.

Another important practice is eliminating habitats suitable for insect survival. You do
this by completely removing plant residues once the production is done. Planting a new
succession directly after the other is not a good idea, hence the importance of having
more than one greenhouse for continual production.

For anything specific, a good resource guide is your best bet. Vegetable MD online from
Cornell University is one example of where to look.

Most greenhouse cucumber varieties have been hybridized not to need pollination.
The cultivars we use are parthenocarpic, and will develop fruits without the help of
pollinators – this is why we can use screens to protect against cucumber beetles and
other insects in the greenhouse.

Cucumber plants will shut down and abort their fruits if temperatures stay above 86 °F
( 30 °C ) for too long during the day. To help the plants cope on very hot days, these
measures will help :

→ Ventilate 24/7 and as much as possible
→ Roll-up sides and all openings should be open to their maximum
→ Install shade cloth over the greenhouse.

When production starts, cucumbers are harvested 3 times a week, and during peak
production, every day. We harvest with tree planting bags and clippers using both of our
hands, clipping the fruits as close as possible to the main stem of the plants, then cutting
the stem closer if necessary. Be sure to remove the flower on the fruit, since it will rot
quickly and make the whole cucumber rot faster. English cukes should be 12-13’’ ( 30 cm )
long and of uniform fullness throughout, and Lebanese cukes should be 5-6’’ ( 13 cm )

APPENDIX 1
GREENHOUSE CUCUMBERS

Just as we do when calculating spacing for greenhouse tomatoes, density for greenhouse
cucumbers is measured by calculating the surface area that is planted and multiplying it
by a density target, measured in square meters ( 1 meter = 3.3 feet ). This is how you figure
out in-row spacing. A standard density target for English cucumbers is 2.2 plants/m2, while
for Lebanese cucumbers the density target should be 3 plants/m2.

English cucumber example :

The greenhouse surface dedicated for cukes is 6 m x 30 m ( 180 m2 ).

Following our 42’’ ( 1.1 m ) bed set-up, cukes are planted every bed, for a total of 6 beds to
be planted in that area.

Aiming for a density of 2.2 heads/m2, we multiply the surface area by the target; 180 m2 x
2.2 heads/m2 = 396 plants on 6 beds, thereby equating to 66 plants/bed.

When transplanting, there are 66 plants/30 metres, meaning 1 plant every 45 cm ( 18” ).

APPENDIX 4
GREENHOUSE CUCUMBERS

Temperature control is the greenhouse grower’s primary tool for adjusting the rate of
development according to overall solar radiation and effects the balance between the
vegetative and generative growth. By playing with day and night temperature settings, you
can steer the plant in a given direction.

Daytime temperature has an effect on cell elongation. A warm day temperature produces
longer internodes and longer leaves. By decreasing this temperature, the opposite
effect will be achieved. For the first part of the day, from sunrise until 9 or 10 am, avoid
excessively hot temperatures, which will cause wilting. Also, a sudden rise in temperatures
in the morning will lead to condensation on the fruits and leaves which is to be avoided,
since it increases the risks of fungal diseases. Overall, the optimal temperature for
photosynthesis is 72 °F ( 22 °C ) ±36 °F ( 2 °C ). Note that optimum fruit production is
reached at a day time temperature of 74-80 °F ( 24-27 °C ) when plants are fully loaded
and have access to enough light.

Night temperature acts mainly on setting and respiration. The optimal temperature for
fruit setting is 64 °F ( 18 °C ) ±34°F ( 1 °C ). Night temperature is more often used to balance
the desired T°24 h. Furthermore, the night T° is set according to the gap required with the
day T°. A large gap between the two promotes generative growth, while little or no gap
between daytime and nighttime temperatures encourages vegetative growth.

Average 24 hour temperature influences the speed at which new flower bunches and
leaves are formed. It also acts on the speed of growth and the ripening of the fruit. Its
increase over a sufficiently long period will accelerate fruit growth, but will lead to a
decrease in fruit size.

APPENDIX 5
GREENHOUSE CUCUMBERS

Pest and disease management in the greenhouse requires research, observation and
careful timing. You need to be able to identify what pests you are dealing with, before
they become an infestation. This is a useful basic guide for scouting your most common
predators in the greenhouse.

Some important pest management strategies include :

→ At the end of each season, remove all plant debris and wash down the greenhouse
interior as much as possible.

→ Install screens on the sides of the greenhouse, under your rollups, to prevent
cucumber beetles and other large insects from entering.

→ At the beginning of the season, grow or buy banker plants to host parasitic wasps
that feed on aphids.

→ Remove all weeds inside and around the greenhouse perimeter - these can provide
an easy habitat for thrips and other pests.

→ Hang yellow sticky cards throughout the crop ( every 5 or 6 plants ) to trap pests.
These are used to monitor whiteflies, thrips, and fungus gnats.

→ Visual inspection of plant leaves. This should be done weekly for signs of two-
spotted mites, aphid infestations and other problems - be sure to check the
undersides of leaves, and inside flowers, where some pests congregate. It can be
useful to uproot a suspicious plant in order to observe its roots and cut its stem to
assess its state of health.

APPENDIX 6
GREENHOUSE CUCUMBERS

In organic production, we grow tomatoes right in the soil. We feed the soil using organic
fertilizers ( compost, feather flour, alfalfa flour, hen manure ). These products need to be
digested by the soil’s fauna and flora ( earthworms, woodlouse, fungi and bacteria ) for
their minerals to be freed and made available to the plants, carried by the soil’s water.
The tomato plants then absorb this water charged with minerals to nourish their tissues
( stems, leaves, fruit, roots ), and to stay healthy and productive.

We depend on the soil’s fauna and flora to feed our crops. If all the critters manage to
function, develop, and reproduce the way they should, the tomato plants will obtain all the
nutritional elements they need to grow.

We can apply tons and tons of composts and other organic fertilizers to the soil, but if the
fauna and flora don’t have the right conditions allowing them to carry out their work, the
minerals will never be made available to the plants. They will end up having small stems,
small clusters and aborted fruit, meaning fewer tomatoes to harvest and sell.

So what exactly are the conditions which bring utter joy to all these little critters ?
Moisture, darkness and a steady supply or organic fertilizers. The tarp helps keep the
fertilizers moist and the soil dark so that the earthworms, woodlouse, and other critters
may gather to feed 24 hours a day within the soil. That is why the plastic has a black
surface.

On the flip side, its white surface serves to increase light reflection onto the plants,
thereby encouraging photosynthesis.
